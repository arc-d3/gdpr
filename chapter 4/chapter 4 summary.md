# Chapter 4 GDPR – Controller and Processor (Art. 24–43)

## Overview

Chapter 4 is the **accountability and governance chapter** — it defines what controllers and processors must actively do to demonstrate compliance, not merely avoid violating rules. It covers the full organisational chain from the controller's own obligations, through contracts with processors, to independent oversight mechanisms such as the DPO and certification. The chapter is organised into five sections:

| Section | Topic | Articles |
|---------|-------|---------|
| 1 | General obligations | Art. 24–31 |
| 2 | Security of personal data | Art. 32–34 |
| 3 | Data protection impact assessment and prior consultation | Art. 35–36 |
| 4 | Data protection officer | Art. 37–39 |
| 5 | Codes of conduct and certification | Art. 40–43 |

---

## Article Summaries

### Art. 24 – Responsibility of the Controller
The controller must implement appropriate **technical and organisational measures (TOMs)** to ensure and demonstrate GDPR compliance, taking into account the nature, scope, context, purposes and risks of processing. Measures must be reviewed and updated as needed. Where proportionate, a formal **data protection policy** must be in place. Adherence to approved codes of conduct (Art. 40) or certification mechanisms (Art. 42) may serve as evidence of compliance.

### Art. 25 – Data Protection by Design and by Default
Controllers must embed data protection at the **design stage** of any system or process and ensure that **by default** only the minimum necessary personal data are processed. Default settings must not expose data to an indefinite number of persons without user action. Certification under Art. 42 can demonstrate compliance with this article.

### Art. 26 – Joint Controllers
Where two or more controllers jointly determine the purposes and means of processing, they are **joint controllers** and must agree (in writing) on their respective compliance responsibilities, including handling data subject rights. The essence of the arrangement must be made available to data subjects, who retain the right to exercise their rights against any of the joint controllers.

### Art. 27 – Representatives of Controllers or Processors Not Established in the Union
Controllers and processors subject to GDPR by virtue of Art. 3(2) (non-EU entities targeting EU data subjects) must **designate a written EU representative** in a relevant Member State. Exceptions apply for occasional low-risk processing and public authorities. The representative acts as the contact point for supervisory authorities and data subjects.

### Art. 28 – Processor
Controllers may only use processors who provide **sufficient guarantees** for GDPR-compliant processing. The relationship must be governed by a **Data Processing Agreement (DPA)** specifying the processor's obligations: process on instructions only, ensure confidentiality, implement security (Art. 32), manage sub-processors, assist with data subject rights and Art. 32–36 obligations, return/delete data, and support audits. Sub-processors require the same contractual obligations. A processor that determines its own purposes and means is reclassified as a controller.

### Art. 29 – Processing Under the Authority of the Controller or Processor
Processors and any person acting under the authority of the controller or processor may process personal data **only on documented instructions** from the controller, unless required otherwise by Union or Member State law.

### Art. 30 – Records of Processing Activities (RoPA)
Controllers must maintain a written **Record of Processing Activities** covering: controller identity, purposes, categories of data subjects/data, recipients, third-country transfers, retention periods, and security measures. Processors maintain their own records. Records must be made available to the supervisory authority on request. Exemption for organisations with fewer than 250 employees unless processing is risky, non-occasional, or involves special categories.

### Art. 31 – Cooperation with the Supervisory Authority
Controllers, processors, and their representatives must **cooperate with the supervisory authority** on request in the performance of its tasks.

### Art. 32 – Security of Processing
Both controllers and processors must implement risk-appropriate **technical and organisational security measures**, including: pseudonymisation and encryption; confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience; recovery capability; regular testing and evaluation. Measures must account for the risk of accidental/unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, or unauthorised access.

### Art. 33 – Notification of a Personal Data Breach to the Supervisory Authority
Controllers must notify the supervisory authority of a personal data breach **within 72 hours** of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to risk rights and freedoms. Processors must notify the controller without undue delay. The notification must describe the breach, its likely consequences, and remediation measures. All breaches must be **documented** internally.

### Art. 34 – Communication of a Personal Data Breach to the Data Subject
Where a breach is likely to result in a **high risk** to individuals, the controller must notify affected data subjects without undue delay. Communication is not required if data are encrypted/unintelligible, if risks have been eliminated, or if disproportionate effort would be involved (public communication instead). The supervisory authority may require notification.

### Art. 35 – Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)
A **DPIA is mandatory** before processing that is likely to result in a high risk, particularly for: systematic profiling with legal effects; large-scale processing of special categories or criminal data; large-scale systematic monitoring of public areas. The DPIA must include a description of the processing, a necessity/proportionality assessment, a risk assessment, and mitigating measures. The DPO must be consulted. Supervisory authorities publish lists of processing requiring/exempt from DPIAs.

### Art. 36 – Prior Consultation
If a DPIA indicates a **residual high risk** that the controller cannot mitigate, the supervisory authority must be consulted before processing commences. The authority has **8 weeks** (extendable by 6 weeks) to provide written advice. Controllers must supply the full DPIA and relevant details. Member States may require prior authorisation for certain public-interest processing.

### Art. 37 – Designation of the Data Protection Officer
A **DPO is mandatory** where: the controller or processor is a public authority; core activities involve regular/systematic large-scale monitoring; or core activities involve large-scale processing of special categories or criminal data. A DPO may be a staff member or external contractor. DPO contact details must be published and communicated to the supervisory authority.

### Art. 38 – Position of the Data Protection Officer
The controller/processor must involve the DPO in all data protection matters, provide necessary resources and data access, and protect the DPO's **independence**: no instructions on tasks, no dismissal or penalty for performing duties, direct reporting to the highest management level. Data subjects may contact the DPO. The DPO is bound by confidentiality.

### Art. 39 – Tasks of the Data Protection Officer
The DPO's minimum tasks: **inform and advise** on GDPR obligations; **monitor compliance** (policies, training, audits); **advise on and monitor DPIAs** (Art. 35); **cooperate** with and act as **contact point** for the supervisory authority; and handle prior consultation (Art. 36). Tasks must be prioritised by risk.

### Art. 40 – Codes of Conduct
Industry associations may develop **sector-specific codes of conduct** to specify how GDPR applies in their context. Codes may cover transparency, legitimate interests, data collection, pseudonymisation, children's data, security, breach notifications, and more. Codes must be approved by the relevant supervisory authority; cross-border codes go through the consistency mechanism and may receive **Union-wide validity** via Commission implementing act.

### Art. 41 – Monitoring of Approved Codes of Conduct
Compliance with an approved code may be monitored by an **accredited monitoring body** (not the supervisory authority itself). The body must be independent, have expertise in the code's subject matter, handle complaints, and avoid conflicts of interest. It can suspend or exclude non-compliant members. Does not apply to public authorities.

### Art. 42 – Certification
Controllers and processors may obtain **voluntary GDPR certification** (seals and marks) to demonstrate compliance. Certification is issued by accredited certification bodies (Art. 43) or the supervisory authority and is valid for a **maximum of 3 years**. The Board may approve criteria leading to a **European Data Protection Seal**. Certification does not reduce the controller's/processor's compliance obligations.

### Art. 43 – Certification Bodies
Certification bodies must be **accredited** by the supervisory authority or a national accreditation body (against EN-ISO/IEC 17065/2012). Requirements include independence, expertise, procedures for issuing/reviewing/withdrawing certifications, complaint procedures, and no conflict of interests. Accreditation is valid for a **maximum of 5 years**. The Commission may adopt delegated/implementing acts on technical standards.
